How we built an HR chatbot without an LLM

Ira answers leave and attendance questions with classic text mining in R. Follow the build in six steps, tour every tab of the app, see what each file does, then learn when an LLM chatbot would be the better choice.

Start with questions and answers

The bot is a lookup system. It recognises what an employee is asking (the intent) and returns the answer HR has already approved. Every distinct answer is one intent, and many wordings point to it. That makes the task a classification problem: text in, intent out.

What the data audit involved

We grouped 238 original questions by identical answer (112 intents), merged 11 duplicates, fixed typos in the answers only, and wrote 660 extra wordings, including typos and short keyword queries. Training questions keep their typos on purpose, so the bot learns to cope with them.

The audit also found answers that contradict each other (advance leave “once a year” versus “any number of times”). A bot repeats whatever it is given, so HR must settle conflicts before launch.
Why not an LLM?

Answers are pre-approved text, so nothing is invented. Every decision can be inspected word by word, which suits teaching and audit. It runs offline, costs nothing per question, and no employee text leaves your machine.

Clean the text

To a computer, “Can’t I carry-forward my EL?” and “cant i carry forward my el” are different strings. Cleaning makes different spellings of one idea look identical. Switch steps on and off and watch the text change. These settings carry over to the next two steps.

    Turn words into numbers

    A document-term matrix has one row per question and one column per word. Each cell counts how often the word appears. TF-IDF then multiplies that count by idf = log2(N / df), where N is the number of questions and df is how many contain the word. Common words such as “leav” get a low weight; rare, telling words such as “encash” get a high one.

    Most cells are empty (sparse). A word the model never saw in training has no column, which is why adding paraphrases helps. The weights for a new question always use the IDF learned from training, never one computed on the question itself.

    Match the question and decide

    Each intent becomes the average (centroid) of its question vectors. A new question is compared with every centroid using cosine similarity: 1 means the same direction, 0 means no shared words. The best score is the bot’s confidence, and two thresholds decide what happens next.

    Worked example: how one question finds its intent

    Picture each intent as a bucket of example questions. A new question is held up against every bucket, and the bucket that shares the most weighty words with it wins. Pick an example and step through the five stages.

    Three ways to score the buckets

    All three methods start from the same cleaned words. They differ in what the new question is compared with. Pick one to see it drawn.

    Prefer words? Read the three methods as text

    Intent centroid (the default): average each bucket’s questions into one point and pick the nearest point, like choosing the closest city centre.

    Nearest questions: compare with every single training question and average the three closest per intent. Good when an intent mixes very different wordings.

    Naive Bayes: for each intent ask “if this were the topic, how likely are these words?”, multiply those chances and compare. It gives probabilities that tend to be extreme, so thresholds are harder to set.

    This demo has 8 intents, so scores run higher than in the real bot (103 intents). The real app also offers Naive Bayes and a nearest-questions method.

    Measure, then improve

    How do we know Ira is any good? We test her like a student: an exam of questions she has never seen. If we marked her on the questions she studied, a bot that simply memorised them would score 100%. Our exam has 105 real HR questions, plus 12 off-topic ones (the weather, a joke) that she should politely refuse.

    The improvement loop

    1. Find the mistakes. Many are genuine neighbours, such as encashing advance leave versus a special day off.
    2. Choose how cautious Ira should be. Use the sliders above. In HR, a wrong answer costs more than “I’m not sure”.
    3. Teach her what she missed. Add three or four wordings to the right intent and retrain.
    Small differences of one to three points are luck, not skill: with 105 questions, one question is about one point.

    LLM chatbot or classic chatbot: which should you choose?

    You have probably used ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. Those are LLM chatbots (LLM stands for large language model). Ira is a classic chatbot. Both rely on machine learning, but they work very differently. This page explains the difference in everyday words, so you can judge which one a business should use.

    Classic chatbot (like Ira)

    Think of a trained receptionist with a binder of approved answers. She listens, finds the page that matches best and reads it out word for word. If nothing matches, she says she is not sure.

    LLM chatbot (like ChatGPT)

    Think of a brilliant freelancer who has read most of the internet. She can discuss almost anything and writes a fresh reply every time, one word at a time. She is fluent and flexible, but sometimes sounds sure when she is wrong.

    Both are machine learning. The real difference is simple: a classic bot finds a ready-made answer, while an LLM bot writes a new one.

    2. Cost: what is a “token”?

    An LLM reads and writes in small pieces called tokens. One token is roughly three-quarters of an English word, so 100 words is about 133 tokens. The company running the LLM charges for every token you send in and every token it writes back, much like a taxi meter. A classic bot has no per-word bill. It still needs a computer to run on and someone to keep the question list up to date.

    4. The best of both: a hybrid bot

    Many companies combine the two. The classic part finds the right approved answer. An LLM then only polishes the wording, or steps in when the classic bot cannot match the question. This is often called RAG (retrieval-augmented generation): the LLM is handed just the right page of the handbook before it writes. You get natural conversation and controlled content, with a much smaller token bill.

    Why Ira is a classic bot: HR answers must be exact, identical for everyone, cheap to run and easy to explain, and students can see every step. That is a textbook case for a classic bot.
    Three common myths

    “LLMs are always smarter, so always better.” They are smarter at language. For exact policy answers, being predictable matters more than being clever.

    “Classic bots are outdated.” Banks, airlines and HR desks still use them because they are cheap, fast and controllable.

    “We can simply tell an LLM never to invent answers.” Instructions reduce mistakes but cannot remove them. That is why serious uses show the source text and add checks.
