What is Classification?

Putting each employee, candidate or case into a labelled bucket, based on patterns learned from past, already-labelled cases.

The core idea: an HRBP's gut feel, made systematic

An experienced HR business partner looks at a file — tenure, workload, pay, engagement score — and thinks "this one is a flight risk." Years of exits she has seen are her training data. The employee file is the new input. Her judgement is the prediction.

A classification model does the same, but learns from thousands of past exits, gives a probability ("73% likely to resign in 6 months"), and then a threshold turns that into YES / NO.
BinaryTwo buckets: Leaves / Stays · Shortlist / Reject · Fraudulent claim / Genuine.
Multi-class3+ buckets: Low / Medium / High performer · Which role suits this candidate?
Threshold = HR decisionAt what risk % do we act? That is a business call, not a maths one (see Tab 05).

📶 A third kind of label: Ordinal categories (buckets with a natural order)

Nominal buckets have no order (Sales / HR / Finance). Ordinal buckets do: performance rating Low < Medium < High, engagement Disengaged < Neutral < Engaged, attrition risk Low < Medium < High. Because there is an order, how far off a wrong guess is matters. Calling a High performer "Low" is a bigger blunder than calling them "Medium". Ordinary classifiers score both as just "wrong"; ordinal methods (ordinal logistic regression, or weighted scoring such as weighted kappa) give partial credit for near misses.

Logistic Regression

Think of it as a scorecard: every factor adds or removes "risk points", and an S-curve converts total points into a probability between 0% and 100%.

📝 The HR scorecard analogy

Imagine rating a resignation risk. Low satisfaction? +points. Heavy overtime? +points. Paid above market? −points. Long tenure? −points. Add them up → one "risk score" (called z). The S-shaped sigmoid squeezes any score into a % chance: very negative score ≈ 0%, zero score = 50%, very positive ≈ 100%.

The equation, in plain English

It looks scary but it is only two lines: (1) add up points, (2) convert points to a percentage.
z = b₀ + b₁×Satisfaction + b₂×Overtime + b₃×PayGap + b₄×Tenure
P(leave) = 1 ÷ (1 + e−z)
z = the risk score: one number summarising how worried we should be. Above 0 means leaning to leave, below 0 leaning to stay.
b₀ = the baseline: the starting score before we look at any factor (like a base mark in an appraisal).
b₁ … b₄ = the weights: how many risk points one unit of that factor adds (+) or removes (−). This is what the model "learns".
e = just a fixed number (≈ 2.72) on a calculator. The formula 1 ÷ (1 + e−z) is a squeezer: it turns any score, however large or small, into a % between 0 and 100.
Risk score z−4−20+2+4
P(leave)2%12%50%88%98%
Memory hook: score 0 = a coin-flip (50%). Every +2 points pushes the chance a long way up; the curve flattens near 0% and 100% because you can never be more than 100% sure.

⚖️ What do the weights mean?

FactorWeightPlain readingEffect on the odds of leaving
Satisfaction (per point)−0.55Each extra satisfaction point removes 0.55 risk points. Negative = protective.×0.58, i.e. about 42% lower odds per point
Overtime (per hour/month)+0.05Each extra overtime hour adds 0.05 risk points. Positive = pushes towards leaving.×1.05 per hour; 10 more hours ≈ ×1.65
Pay below market (per 1%)+0.07Each 1% pay gap adds 0.07 risk points.×1.07 per 1%; a 10% gap ≈ ×2.0 (odds double)
Tenure (per year)−0.12Each year of service removes 0.12 points: long-serving staff are stickier.×0.89 per year, about 11% lower odds

How to read a weight: the sign says the direction (+ pushes towards leaving, − protects), the size says how strongly per unit. Since units differ (points vs hours vs %), compare weights fairly only after converting every factor to the same scale.

Where do weights come from? The model starts with random weights, scores all past employees, checks how often it guessed their exit/stay correctly, and nudges the weights again and again until the guesses match history as closely as possible. It is like tuning an appraisal scorecard until it would have ranked past leavers correctly.

Caution: weights show association, not proof of cause. Overtime may be linked to exits because of an overloaded team, not overtime itself.

Decision Trees & Random Forest

A tree asks yes/no questions that make each group less mixed-up, until every group is pure.

Step 0 · Impurity = "how mixed-up is this group?"

Take a group of past employees. If half left and half stayed, you cannot guess an outcome: maximum confusion. If all left (or all stayed), there is nothing to guess: no confusion. Data scientists call this mix-up impurity. There are two popular rulers to measure it: Entropy and Gini impurity. Both read 0 for a pure group and rise as the group gets more mixed.
6 left · 6 stayedEntropy = 1.00
Gini = 0.50
Total confusion
8 left · 2 stayedEntropy = 0.72
Gini = 0.32
Mostly one way
5 left · 0 stayedEntropy = 0
Gini = 0
Pure: stop asking

Ruler 1 · Entropy = a "surprise score"

In a 50:50 group every pick is a surprise. In an all-left group nothing surprises you. The more surprise, the higher the entropy.
Entropy = −p·log₂(p) − q·log₂(q)  (p = share who left, q = share who stayed)

Ruler 2 · Gini impurity = "chance of a wrong guess"

Pick one person at random, then guess "left" or "stayed" using the group's own mix. How often are you wrong? In a group of 8 left and 2 stayed: you pick a leaver (80%) and wrongly guess "stayed" (20%), which is 16% of the time; or you pick a stayer (20%) and wrongly guess "left" (80%), another 16%. Wrong 32% of the time, so Gini = 0.32.
Gini = 1 − (p² + q²)  (for two groups this is the same as 2 × p × q)

How the tree uses them

Information Gain = Entropy before the question − average Entropy after it
Gini Gain = Gini before the question − average Gini after it
"Average" means bigger groups count more. Gain is "how much confusion did this question remove?" At every step the tree asks the question with the highest gain. The two rulers use different scales (Entropy tops out at 1.00, Gini at 0.50) but nearly always choose the same question. Gini is a little quicker to compute, so many tools use it by default.

🎒 Bagging = Bootstrap AGGregating

One tree can memorise quirks of its data. A Random Forest fixes this with three ideas:
1. Bootstrap: each tree gets its own "bag": 12 employees drawn at random with replacement, so some appear twice and some (about ⅓) are left out ("out-of-bag").
2. Random features: each tree may only look at 2 of the 4 factors, so the trees think differently.
3. Aggregate: all trees vote; the majority wins. Like an interview panel where each member sees different parts of the file.

Four more ways to draw the line. KNN is the easiest to see, so we play with it.

K = number of neighbours to ask3

📧 Naive BayesCounts how often words appear in each category. If grievance emails with "salary", "delay", "manager" were mostly Payroll cases, a new email with those words is sent to Payroll. Super fast, great for text.
✂️ SVMDraws the line with the widest safe gap between "left" and "stayed". Useful when there are many features, e.g. words in survey comments.
⚡ XGBoostBuilds small trees one after another; each new tree focuses on the employees the previous trees got wrong. Often the most accurate, but harder to explain to a CHRO.

📉 How many neighbours? The elbow graph INTERACTIVE

We test each K by hiding one employee at a time, asking the model to guess whether they left, and checking the guess against what really happened. The up-and-down (vertical) axis is simply "how many guesses were wrong out of every 100 employees". This is called the error rate (the misclassification rate). It is the exact opposite of accuracy: error % + accuracy % = 100%, so 15% error means 85% accuracy. "%" just means "out of every 100".
Tiny K is jumpy and copies noise; huge K blurs real differences. Pick the K where the wrong guesses stop falling meaningfully: the elbow, or the lowest point (prefer the smaller K when tied: simpler). Click a dot to see its numbers.
Show the up-axis as:
K=1 makes 13% errors; the lowest error (7%) is at K = 19, but the curve is already almost flat from K = 3 (green dot, error 8%), the elbow. Beyond it extra neighbours add little, so choose the smaller, simpler K.

Naive Bayes

A detective's method: start with a hunch, update it after every clue. Very fast, and brilliant with words.

Step 1 · Conditional probability: "among those who have X, how many have Y?"

Here are 100 past employees. Red = left, green = stayed, black ring = had heavy overtime.

The tree is the same 100 people in boxes. Ignore everyone without a ring: only 38 people had heavy overtime and 24 of them left. So P(Left | Heavy overtime) = 24 ÷ 38 = 63%. Read "|" as "given that".

Step 2 · Bayes' theorem, in plain words

Exit records tell us "what do leavers look like?" (80% of leavers had heavy overtime). But HR needs the reverse: "this person has heavy overtime, will they leave?" Bayes flips the question:
P(Left | sign) = P(sign | Left) × P(Left) ÷ P(sign)
P(Left) = hunch before any clue: 30 of 100 leave = 0.30
P(sign | Left) = how common the sign is among leavers: 24 ÷ 30 = 0.80
P(sign) = how common the sign is in everyone: 38 ÷ 100 = 0.38
Answer: 0.80 × 0.30 ÷ 0.38 = 0.63, the same 63% we counted on the tree.

Step 3 · "Naive" = treat every clue as independent

With several clues we multiply their strengths. Each clue has a push (likelihood ratio): how much more common it is among leavers than stayers. Push above ×1 nudges towards LEAVE, below ×1 towards STAY. It is "naive" because heavy overtime and low satisfaction often travel together, yet the shortcut works remarkably well.

Example 2 · Count first, then multiply: will this employee quit?

Same idea, but with a table small enough to count by hand: 14 past employees, 9 of whom quit. Picture two teams: the Quit team (9 people) and the Stay team (5 people). When a new employee arrives, we ask each team one question: "How much does this person look like one of yours?" The team that fits better wins.
① Count the people  →  ② Turn counts into fractions  →  ③ Multiply the fractions  →  ④ Bigger score wins

🔍 Warm-up · Conditional probability INTERACTIVE

A conditional probability answers one question: "Among only the people in a chosen group, what fraction have a certain trait?" The bar | simply reads "given that": first shrink the crowd to the group after the bar, then count. Order matters: "Of those who quit, how many were Married?" is a different question from "Of the Married, how many quit?"

✨ The kernel trick INTERACTIVE

HR twist: weekly overtime vs normal. Very low (disengaged) and very high (burnout) both leave; moderate staff stay. On one line no single cut separates them. Lift each point up by its square (x²) and a flat line works.

Choose SVM whenMany features vs few rows (survey comments, CV text) · classes form a clear gap · you do not need probabilities.

The Threshold: an HR business decision

The model gives a % risk. You decide at what % to act. Too low → you waste time on people who were fine. Too high → you miss people who then resign.

💰 The two kinds of mistake

Missed leaver (False Negative): model said safe, employee resigned → costs ~₹6,00,000.
False alarm (False Positive): model said risky, employee was fine → a wasted stay-conversation, ~₹5,000 of manager time.
Because a miss costs 120× more than a false alarm, HR usually prefers a lower threshold.

🪣 Logistic Regression
Choose when:
  • You need a probability and readable factor weights
  • Roughly straight-line relationships
  • Small to medium data; a baseline first
  • Regulators or CHRO want transparency
🪣 Decision Tree
Choose when:
  • You must explain each decision as if-then rules
  • Mixed numbers and categories
  • Rules a manager can follow without a computer
  • Small data; one tree can overfit
🪣 Random Forest
Choose when:
  • A single tree is too unstable
  • Medium to large tabular data, many noisy features
  • You want feature importance
  • Accuracy over explainability
🪣 Naive Bayes
Choose when:
  • Text or word-count data
  • Many features, little data
  • You need something fast and simple
  • Clues roughly independent
🪣 KNN
Choose when:
  • The question is "who is most similar to…?"
  • Small data, few features, on the same scale
  • No model to train, just compare
  • Slow on very large data
🪣 SVM
Choose when:
  • Many features but few labelled rows
  • A clear gap between the groups
  • Curved patterns (use a kernel)
  • You do not need probabilities
🪣 XGBoost
Choose when:
  • Large messy tabular data, 100s of columns
  • Highest accuracy matters most
  • Missing values and interactions
  • Explainability is secondary
