2 · How pay is really structured: grades, bands and compa-ratio

Pay is not decided from scratch for each person. Every job sits in a grade, and every grade has a salary band. Any salary under discussion is compared with the grade’s band.

🏷️ Grade

The level of the job (G1 Associate … G7 Associate Director). It sets which band applies.

📐 Salary band

A minimum, midpoint and maximum for the grade. The midpoint is the “market-rate for a fully competent person”.

🎯 Compa-ratio

Salary ÷ Midpoint
1.00 = paid exactly at midpoint. 0.90 = 10% below. 1.12 = 12% above. It makes pay comparable across grades.

📏 Range penetration

(Salary − Min) ÷ (Max − Min)
How far through the band the person has travelled: 0% = at minimum, 100% = at maximum.

🌐 Market ratio

Salary ÷ market median for the grade and city. Tells you whether you pay above or below the outside market.
Every employee against their grade’s band (box = min to max, thick line = midpoint; red = outside the band)
Distribution of compa-ratio
Green: 0.90–1.10 (the normal zone). Amber: 0.80–0.90 or 1.10–1.20. Red: outside. Most people cluster near 1.0, which is how a healthy structure looks.

4 · Feature engineering: turning raw columns into signals

A “feature” is any column the model learns from. Feature engineering reshapes raw data so the pattern becomes visible: HR data translated into the language the model speaks.

Blue headers = raw columns from the HRIS. Green headers = engineered features created from them.
Lookup: grade → band midpoint“G4” is a label, not a quantity. Replacing it with the band midpoint (₹18L) gives the model the real size of the grade. Grades are not equal steps: bands grow ~35% per grade.
One-hot encodingText like “MBA” becomes yes/no columns (edu_MBA = 1). Education is not 1, 2, 3 in rupee terms.
Grouping8 cities become 3 tiers; fewer, meaningful groups give the model more examples each.
Relative-to-gradeexperience − typical experience of the grade: a 12-year G3 is “ahead of grade”, so likely higher in the band.
CappingTime in grade capped at 8 years: beyond that, extra years add no more pay.
Log transformlog(1+reports) stops a manager of 60 from drowning everyone else.
PolynomialExperience², Grade² let the model bend: fast growth early, flat later; steeper at higher grades.
Ratio targetInstead of predicting ₹ salary, predict the compa-ratio. Drivers like performance are percentages of the band, not fixed rupees. This is Model 5.
🚫 Never use these as inputs: compa-ratio, range penetration, market ratio, total target cash. They are calculated from salary, so the model would be reading the answer sheet (data leakage). Likewise gender is never a predictor; it is used only in the pay-equity audit (section 8).

5b · Reading the errors like a CHRO (no maths needed)

Every model makes mistakes. The question is not “is it perfect?” but “how wrong is it, in rupees, and is that good enough for the decision?” Pick a model and everything below speaks about it in plain English.

📏 An “error”

The gap between the salary the model predicted and what the person actually earns. Model says ₹22L, person earns ₹25L → error = ₹3L (under-predicted).

🚗 Think of a GPS

MAE = how many minutes off the arrival time usually is. RMSE = the same, but it punishes the occasional 40-minute jam extra hard. R² = how much of the trip-time variation the GPS actually understands.

🎯 The “dumb” baseline

The laziest model guesses the average salary for everyone (R² = 0). Every real model is judged on how much better it does than this lazy guess.

7 · Pay-equity audit (gender is checked, never predicted)

Gender is deliberately not in any model. We use it only afterwards to ask: do similar people sit in the same place in their bands, regardless of gender?

Raw vs adjusted gap (men minus women, in compa-ratio points)
Average compa-ratio by grade
💡 Raw gap = simple difference in averages. Adjusted gap = difference after comparing like with like (same grade, performance, location, hire source, experience vs grade…). An adjusted gap whose range excludes zero is a signal to investigate (e.g. starting-salary negotiation, promotion timing), not proof of discrimination.

9 · Under the hood: Cross-validation

If one split is partly luck, use all of them. Cross-validation cuts the data into k folds. Each fold takes one turn as the hidden test group while the others train. Average the k scores for a steadier verdict.

10 · Under the hood: overfitting, bias and variance

A model that is too simple misses the pattern (high bias). One that is too flexible memorises noise (high variance). The sweet spot sits between.

🥶 Too simple (underfit)
Like pricing every role at the company average. Errors are high on both Train and Test.
✅ Just right
Captures real patterns (grade band, location, hire source). Train ≈ Test.
🌀 Too flexible (overfit)
Learns quirks of the 700 training people. Train error tiny; Test/CV error rises.
Complexity dial: experience polynomial degree 1–6
Train error keeps falling with every bend; cross-validated error stops improving early. Extra bends buy nothing.
Learning curve: does more data help?
With few employees, Train is tiny and Test is large (memorising). As data grows the lines converge. The gap tells you whether to collect more data or simplify.

12 · What do these scores mean?

R² — “how much is explained”Share of pay differences between employees the model accounts for. 0 = no better than the average; 1 = perfect.
RMSE — “typical miss, harsh on big misses”Average error in ₹ lakh, with large errors counted extra. Use when big pay errors are costly.
MAE — “average miss”Average size of the error ignoring direction. Easiest to explain to a CHRO.
Train vs Test vs CVTrain = practice papers, Test = the exam, CV = several mock exams averaged. Trust the Test/CV numbers.
