01

What is Text Mining & NLP?

Every day, your organisation produces oceans of unstructured text — customer emails, product reviews, call-centre transcripts, social media posts, contracts, résumés. Text Mining is the process of turning that messy text into structured data a computer (and a spreadsheet) can work with. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the broader field of teaching computers to read, interpret and even generate human language.

Business analogy: Think of raw customer reviews as an unsorted warehouse of boxes. Text mining is the process of opening every box, labelling the contents, and stacking them onto shelves (rows and columns) so a manager can ask questions like "how many boxes mention 'late delivery'?" in seconds.

Why a business student should care

Customer intelligence

Mine thousands of reviews to find the top 5 complaints in minutes instead of reading each one.

Risk & compliance

Scan contracts or emails automatically for risky clauses or fraud language.

HR & hiring

Match résumés to job descriptions at scale.

Marketing

Track brand sentiment on social media in real time.

03

Stopwords

Stopwords are extremely common words — "the", "is", "and", "of" — that carry grammatical structure but very little topical meaning. Removing them shrinks the vocabulary and lets analysis focus on words that actually distinguish one document from another.

Business analogy: If you asked ten customers "what did you think of the product?", almost every sentence would contain "the", "was", "and". Counting those words tells you nothing about satisfaction — but counting "broken", "excellent" or "late" does.

04

Stemming vs Lemmatization

Words like "run", "running" and "ran" all refer to the same underlying concept. Reducing them to one form shrinks vocabulary size and groups related words together.

Stemming

A fast, rule-based process that chops off common suffixes (-ing, -ed, -es...). It is quick but crude — it can produce forms that are not real words.

Lemmatization

A dictionary-aware process that returns the real base word ("lemma") using grammar and vocabulary — slower, but more accurate.

Simulation — compare both on the same words

The stemmer is the genuine Porter (1980) algorithm — the same rules NLTK uses. The lemmatizer is a small teaching dictionary plus suffix rules; production tools (spaCy, NLTK-WordNet) use full dictionaries and part-of-speech tags.

What to notice with the default words: the stemmer turns studies into “studi” (not a real word), organization into “organ” (a different meaning — over-stemming), and customers into “custom” (merging two different ideas). It also leaves better, mice, children and went untouched because they are irregular. The lemmatizer, using a dictionary, returns good, mouse, child, go and keeps organization intact. Speed vs accuracy: stemming is faster, lemmatization is more correct.

05

Tokens & N-grams

A token is one unit of text — usually a word. An n-gram is a sequence of "n" consecutive tokens. Single words (unigrams) lose word order; bigrams and trigrams capture short phrases like "customer service" or "not happy" that carry more meaning together than apart.

Simulation — generate n-grams

Notice how "not respond" (a bigram) signals a complaint far more clearly than "not" and "respond" counted separately.

Where n-grams show up in real products

Search & autocomplete

Google/Amazon search-bar suggestions are powered by which n-grams historically follow what you've typed so far.

Keyboard prediction

Your phone's "next word" suggestion is a language model trained on bigram/trigram frequencies from millions of messages.

Key-phrase extraction

Pulling "money back guarantee" or "customer service" out of reviews as a single unit, rather than three separate, less meaningful words.

Plagiarism & duplicate detection

Comparing documents by shared n-grams catches copied phrasing that a single-word (unigram) comparison would miss entirely.

06

Bag of Words vs Semantic Parsing

So far every technique has treated a sentence as an unordered pile of words — that's the Bag of Words (BoW) philosophy: fast, simple, and good enough for many business tasks. But BoW cannot tell "the bank raised interest rates" apart from "interest raised the bank rates" — it loses grammar entirely. Semantic parsing (and its narrower cousin, dependency parsing) keeps the sentence's structure: who did what, to whom, and how.

Bag of Words view

A flat list of word counts. No idea which word acted on which. Cheap to compute, easy to scale to millions of documents — the foundation of DTM, TF-IDF and cosine similarity.

Semantic / dependency parsing view

Identifies grammatical roles — subject, verb, object — and how words depend on each other. Expensive to compute, but understands relationships and meaning, not just presence.

How dependency parsing actually works, in three steps

1 · POS tagging
Label every word's part of speech — noun, verb, adjective, determiner...
→
2 · Grammatical roles
Find the verb (the "root" action), then work out who is doing it (subject) and who/what receives it (object).
→
3 · Dependency tree
Draw arcs linking each dependent word back to the word it modifies — the structure a machine can now reason over.
NOUN — thing/person VERB / AUX — action PRON — pronoun DET — the/a/this ADJ — describes a noun ADV — describes a verb

Where this precision actually matters in business

Contract & legal review

"The vendor shall indemnify the client" vs "the client shall indemnify the vendor" — identical Bag-of-Words, opposite legal obligation. Contract-analytics tools must parse structure, not just keywords.

Voice assistants

"Book a flight to Paris from London" vs "...to London from Paris" — a BoW model sees the same words; the assistant needs the dependency structure to book the correct route.

Chatbot slot-filling

Extracting entities correctly (who is the sender, who is the receiver of a payment) relies on grammatical roles, not just detecting names in the sentence.

Coreference & pronouns

"The agent apologized to the customer because she was upset" — resolving who "she" refers to needs structural, not just word-count, analysis.

Business analogy: "Customer service failed the customer" and "the customer failed customer service" contain identical words — a BoW/DTM model scores them as 100% similar. A semantic parser reads the roles and knows these mean opposite things. This is why serious chatbots, contract-review tools and voice assistants combine both: BoW/TF-IDF for speed and scale across millions of documents, semantic parsing for precision on the individual sentences that matter most.
07

The Document-Term Matrix (DTM)

Building on the Bag-of-Words idea from Module 6: laying every document's word-counts side by side produces the Document-Term Matrix: one row per document, one column per unique word (term), and each cell holding how many times that word appears in that document.

Business analogy: Imagine three customer complaint emails. A DTM is exactly like a spreadsheet where each row is one email and each column is a word such as "refund", "late" or "broken" — the cells simply count how many times each word shows up in each email. This turns free text into numbers a model can compute with.

TF-IDF: Weighing Words by Importance

Raw counts have a flaw: a word appearing in every document (like “product”) isn't distinctive, while a word appearing in only one document (like “damaged”) is a strong signal. TF-IDF (Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency) re-weights the DTM so rare-but-relevant words score higher than common-but-uninformative ones. This module shows every number, step by step — nothing is hidden.

Business analogy — the highlighter test: Imagine highlighting the most telling words in each of 100 exit-interview notes. Words like “the” or “manager” (in nearly every note) would be useless highlights. A word like “micromanagement” that shows up often in one note but in almost no others is exactly what you would highlight. TF-IDF is that highlighter, done with arithmetic: TF says “how much does this note talk about the word?”, IDF says “how unusual is the word across all notes?”.

The three ingredients

1 · TF — Term Frequency
TF(t, d) = count of t in d ÷ total words in d

How prominent is the word inside this one document? Dividing by length keeps long documents from winning just because they have more words.

2 · IDF — Inverse Document Frequency
IDF(t) = ln( N ÷ df )

N = number of documents. df = number of documents that contain the word (at least once). The fewer documents use the word, the bigger the IDF. ln is the natural logarithm (calculator key “ln”).

3 · TF-IDF
TF-IDF = TF × IDF

High score = frequent in this document and rare in the others. A word in every document gets IDF = 0, so its TF-IDF is 0 whatever its TF.

How IDF behaves (a corpus of N = 10 documents)

Word appears in…dfN ÷ dfIDF = ln(N ÷ df)Meaning
1 document110.02.303Very distinctive — a signature word
2 documents25.01.609Distinctive
5 documents52.00.693Common
10 documents101.00.000In every document — carries no information
Two versions of IDF you will meet. Classic (above): ln(N ÷ df). Smoothed (scikit-learn's default): ln((1+N) ÷ (1+df)) + 1 — the “+1”s guarantee no word ever gets exactly zero and avoid dividing by zero for unseen words. Some textbooks write ln(N ÷ (1+df)); that can go negative for very common words, which is why libraries moved to the smoothed form. The base of the logarithm (ln, log₁₀, log₂) changes the numbers but not which word ranks higher. Use the selector below to switch and watch every table update.

Document Similarity (Cosine Similarity)

Once documents are numeric vectors (via TF-IDF), we can measure how "close" two documents are by the angle between their vectors — this is cosine similarity, ranging from 0 (completely different topics) to 1 (identical wording pattern). This single idea powers search engines, recommendation systems and résumé matching.

The formula — and why the angle matters

cosine similarity(A, B) = (A · B) ÷ ( |A| × |B| )

A · B (dot product): multiply the two documents' TF-IDF weights word by word, then add them up. Only words found in both documents contribute.
|A| (length): √(sum of A's squared weights). Dividing by the lengths means a long document is not “more similar” just because it has bigger numbers — only the pattern of words counts.

Small angle θ → cos θ close to 1 → similar. 90° → cos = 0 → nothing in common.

Topic Modeling

So far each document has been compared word-for-word. Topic modeling goes one layer deeper: it assumes every document is really a mixture of hidden themes ("topics"), and every topic is a mixture of words that tend to co-occur. Instead of reading 10,000 reviews, a manager can ask "what are the 3 underlying themes, and how much of each does each review contain?"

Business analogy: Feed in a year of customer feedback and a topic model might surface three recurring themes — "delivery & logistics", "product quality", "customer support" — without you ever telling it those categories exist. It discovers them purely from which words tend to appear together.

Simulation — discover topics with Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)

NMF is a simplified, teaching-friendly cousin of LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation) — the algorithm most production topic-modeling tools use. Both share the same core idea: decompose Documents × Words into Documents × Topics and Topics × Words.

NMF starts from random weights, so a single run can land on a mediocre solution. Each click therefore tries 8 random starts and keeps the one with the lowest reconstruction error; small differences between clicks are a genuine property of NMF/LDA, not a bug.

Sentiment & Emotion Analysis

Not all sentiment tools are equal, and a single positive/negative label often hides more than it reveals. Explore four widely-taught approaches side by side, from simplest to most nuanced.

The most basic method: a dictionary of positive/negative words, summed up. It cannot tell "good" from "not good" or "very good" apart, and it scores each document as a single number with no explanation of why.

VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary for sEntiment Reasoning) was built specifically for social media text. Unlike a plain lexicon, it adjusts scores for negation ("not good"), intensifiers ("very good", "extremely bad"), punctuation emphasis ("great!!!") and ALL-CAPS shouting.

Simple lexicon says

VADER-style says

This is a simplified re-implementation of VADER's core rules for teaching purposes — the real VADER (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014) uses a hand-validated lexicon of over 7,500 terms plus emoji and slang support. It is the go-to tool for tweets, app-store reviews and chat transcripts precisely because that text is full of punctuation and capitalisation cues.

The NRC Word-Emotion Association Lexicon (Mohammad & Turney) goes beyond positive/negative — it maps words onto 8 core emotions (Plutchik's wheel): joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation. This reveals why a review is negative — anger? disappointment? fear?

Uses a compact illustrative subset of the NRC lexicon covering common business/review vocabulary — the full public lexicon covers roughly 14,000 English words. Brand teams use this to tell "annoyed" apart from "genuinely afraid to keep using the product" — very different responses are needed.

Aspect-based sentiment analysis (ABSA) solves the single-score problem: a review can be positive about one thing and negative about another in the same sentence. Instead of one score per document, it finds each mentioned aspect (delivery, service, quality, price...) and scores sentiment around that aspect only.

Clause-by-clause
Sentiment per aspect

This illustrative version splits the sentence at commas/"but"/"however" and matches a small aspect keyword dictionary (delivery, service, quality, price, packaging). Production ABSA systems use trained models to find aspects and their linked opinion words even without such obvious clause breaks.

Where sentiment & emotion analysis is used in business

Support ticket triage

Auto-flag angry or fearful customers (NRC) for priority human response, while routine positive/neutral tickets stay in the standard queue.

Brand & social monitoring

Track sentiment on X/Instagram in near real time to catch a PR problem within hours instead of at next quarter's survey.

Product/feature-level feedback

Aspect-based sentiment tells a product team "battery life" is loved but "app stability" is hated — one aggregate score never could.

Employee experience (HR)

The same lexicons run on internal survey text to flag burnout language early, well before it shows up in attrition numbers.

A limit worth knowing: none of these tools reliably catch sarcasm. "Oh great, another delay" scores positive to a simple lexicon (it sees "great") and often still confuses VADER, because the words themselves are positive — only real-world context reveals the sarcasm. This is exactly why human review still matters for high-stakes decisions, and why modern production systems increasingly use large language models (which read context, not just individual words) for the hardest cases.

Emoji & Emoticon Analytics

Employees and candidates rarely write only words. In LinkedIn comments, WhatsApp pulse polls and chat tools like Slack or Teams, emojis carry emotion (😍 😡 🙄 👍). A text-only model ignores them and can misread the message. Emoji analytics converts them into data.

HR analogy: Reading a comment without its emojis is like judging an interview by the transcript alone, without tone of voice or body language.

Cautions specific to emojis

Sarcasm

"Great, another Monday 😂" is positive in words and emoji, but the intent may be negative.

Changing meaning

Meanings drift by age group and community (💀 can mean "I'm laughing"). 🙏 can mean thanks, please or prayer.

Platform and skin tone

Rendering differs by device; skin-tone and sequence emojis must be normalised before counting.

Representativeness

Emoji users are not all employees. Treat results as signals, not proof.
